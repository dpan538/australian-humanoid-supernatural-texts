# Results Draft

## 4 Results

The paper freeze records a local repository state rather than a live website capture. The freeze was generated as `paper_hss_discovery_not_evidence_20260706` and writes aggregate outputs under `data/releases/paper_hss_discovery_not_evidence_20260706/`. The freeze manifest records git commit `b1f339d0672295aaa89ce678fe624510fea4b4dd`, a dirty local worktree count of 419 files at the time of the freeze, and checksums for the configured inputs. The dirty worktree note is important for interpretation: the paper freeze is reproducible through recorded file checksums, but it should not be represented as a clean tagged repository release until a publication release is made. The freeze also records that no raw source text, HTML, XML, PDFs, Word files, cookies, credentials, or SQLite database copies were placed in the paper release.

The generated count reconciliation confirms that the archive contains multiple non-interchangeable count families. In the local frontend export, `public/data/frontend-data.json` contains 4,265 frontend records, 1,593 map points, and 1,593 map flags. The equality of map points and map flags is recorded as a Boolean count value of 1 in the generated stats, supporting the local export invariant that each public map point has a corresponding public map flag. These are local frontend export counts, not live deployed website counts. Live public website display counts for records and mapped records are marked "not available in current local data" and should not be inferred from the static export without a separate deployed-site capture.

The legacy and V2 corpus counts differ because they measure different objects. The legacy flat `records` table contains 4,638 records, including 1,097 records with a `full_text_path`. The V2 normalized layer contains 4,526 source items and 4,457 narrative units. Of those narrative units, 4,444 are public-display eligible under the generated rule of `display_mode` in `full`, `summary_only`, or `metadata_only`, with `analysis_status` not excluded. The V2 layer also reports 4,457 narrative source links. By contrast, the local `source_chains` SQLite table contains 0 rows, while the source-chain audit file reports 4,526 source-chain audit rows, 0 missing original-source-name rows, and 898 tier-E or discovery-like rows. This is a data-structure warning rather than a substantive contradiction: source-chain audit outputs exist, but the dedicated `source_chains` table is not populated in the freeze.

The strict no-credential record-gate experiment produced 0 strict target-gap records under the configured gate, against a configured target of 2,000 records. The surrounding experiment tables show 2,762 provisional records, 94 harvest pages, 7,200 harvest candidates, 22 structured endpoints, 120 structured endpoint records, 120 structured near misses, and 240 structured enriched records. These counts show that the strict gate did not fail because no traces were visible. It failed because the visible traces did not satisfy all record requirements simultaneously under the configured constraints. The result therefore supports the paper's central argument: discovery and access are not evidentiary adequacy.

Lead mode retained the traces that strict record mode could not promote. The generated lead-mode count is 21,343 target-gap leads, and the configuration records lead mode as enabled. A priority threshold of score greater than or equal to 80 identifies 10,581 priority-scored leads. These leads are not public records, not public map flags, and not evidence-source rows. They are reviewable public-text traces with blockers, source-family labels where available, route families, duplicate statuses, and recommended review actions. In methodological terms, the archive converts unstable discovery into structured review work rather than discarding it or weakening record standards.

The blocker distribution identifies where the strict gate failed. Six blocker classes are present in the generated `lead_blocker_counts.csv`. The largest category is `missing_date`, with 10,320 leads, or 48.35% of target-gap leads. The next largest is `missing_item_url`, with 6,000 leads, or 28.11%. `missing_term` accounts for 3,863 leads, or 18.1%. Smaller but methodologically important categories include `strict_record_gate_not_met` with 629 leads, or 2.95%; `ethics_sensitive` with 431 leads, or 2.02%; and `robots_unknown` with 100 leads, or 0.47%. These results show that blockers are not only technical access errors. They include temporal absence, item-level identity, semantic signal, ethics review, and permission uncertainty.

Missingness in the target-gap leads reinforces the blocker analysis. The generated stats report 10,769 leads missing temporal signal, 14,361 missing term signal, 11,092 missing place signal, and 10,950 missing source family. Expressed as generated shares, these correspond to 50.46%, 67.29%, 51.97%, and 51.3% respectively. These fields are central to the proposed provenance gate. Their absence means that many public-text traces cannot responsibly be promoted to records without additional review, source-chain reconstruction, or policy decisions.

Source-family concentration is visible in both public-map outputs and lead outputs. In the frontend map source-family table, `AYR_FAMILY` accounts for 1,272 map rows, or 79.85% of frontend map rows. The next source families are much smaller: Australian Broadcasting Corporation has 116 rows, or 7.28%; `INTERNET_ARCHIVE_FAMILY` has 74 rows, or 4.65%; `GUTENBERG_FAMILY` has 68 rows, or 4.27%; and State Library Victoria has 21 rows, or 1.32%. This distribution should not be interpreted as a distribution of cultural tradition, narrative occurrence, or population. It is a distribution of public-map display rows by source-family mediation in the local frontend export. It shows how a small number of source families can dominate visible public-map surfaces.

Lead source-family counts require additional caution because missingness is the largest lead source-family bucket. The generated lead source-family table records 10,950 leads, or 51.3%, with source family missing. Among non-missing source-family rows, the largest categories are `museum_heritage_page` with 1,972 leads, or 9.24%; `heritage_register` with 1,932 leads, or 9.05%; `state_library_catalogue` with 1,496 leads, or 7.01%; `council_local_studies` with 1,457 leads, or 6.83%; `local_history_serial` with 1,431 leads, or 6.7%; and `state_archive_catalogue` with 1,088 leads, or 5.1%. These rankings should be read as fielded lead metadata within the paper freeze, not as complete source-family coverage.

The source organization and source type tables further show concentration in the normalized source layer. `source_items.publication_or_organisation` contains 315 distinct source organizations. The largest source organization is Australian Yowie Research with 898 source items, or 19.84%. The source type table contains 26 distinct source types, with `repository_full_text` as the largest type at 1,552 source items, or 34.29%. Other leading source types include public-domain ebooks with 968 source items, or 21.39%, and modern web with 905 source items, or 20.0%. These tables support the article's claim that public-text archives are shaped by access infrastructures and source-family mediation, not only by the underlying cultural materials being indexed.

The manual audit sample has been generated but not yet coded. The sample protocol requested 100 leads, wrote 100 leads, used deterministic random seed 538, and sampled from 21,343 candidate leads before the sensitivity filter. The redaction rule excluded 544 sensitive-looking rows. The sample includes only review metadata and URL domains, with blank human coding columns for source-chain completeness, original-source identifiability, evidence-source adequacy, date availability, term availability, source-stated place availability, ethics sensitivity, record eligibility, map eligibility, reasons for ineligibility, and reviewer notes. Map and display findings confirm the importance of count separation. The generated stats report 4,265 canonical frontend public records and 1,593 canonical frontend public map rows from the count reconciliation file. A separate local-rule calculation identifies 1,098 map-eligible narrative-location rows from the internal join of `narrative_locations`, `locations`, and `narrative_units`, with a 24.99% local-rule eligibility share over 4,393 internal narrative-location rows. The generated stats explicitly warn that the local-rule map-eligible internal rows should not be substituted for the frontend map count. This distinction matters because the frontend map is a public display surface, while internal narrative locations include broader review and modelling rows.

Overall, the results support the methodological claim that provenance-aware public-text archives should preserve distinctions among discovery, access, origin, evidence, lead status, record status, and display status. The strict gate did not yield target-gap records, but lead mode retained a large reviewable population with explicit blockers. The public-map source-family distribution is concentrated, but that concentration is a property of archival visibility and source mediation, not a factual geography. The strongest unresolved empirical gaps are the absence of live website counts in the paper freeze, incomplete manual-audit coding, missing source-family fields for many leads, and the empty `source_chains` SQLite table in spite of generated source-chain audit outputs.
